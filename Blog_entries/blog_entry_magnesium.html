Magnesium: the mineral almost all of us are short on

Why subclinical magnesium deficiency is so common, which form to choose, and why I save it for before bed.

Magnesium has been documented to participate in more than 300 enzymatic reactions: protein synthesis, glucose regulation, muscle and nerve function, cellular energy production (ATP), DNA and RNA synthesis. It is, at some distance from potassium, the most abundant intracellular mineral alongside sodium. And despite that — or perhaps because of it, since its functions are so diffuse — its deficiency frequently goes unnoticed.

Subclinical magnesium deficiency does not produce specific, easy-to-recognise symptoms. It produces chronic fatigue, irritability, nocturnal cramps, difficulty falling asleep, tension headaches, a persistent sense of muscular tension. Things attributed to stress, to pace of life, to "I just don't sleep well". Sometimes that is the reason. Sometimes it may also be a degree of magnesium insufficiency.

Why citrate

There are many forms of magnesium: oxide, citrate, malate, glycinate, threonate, taurate. Oxide is the cheapest and the poorest absorbed; it also has a potent laxative effect that many find uncomfortable. Citrate has reasonable bioavailability, good gastrointestinal tolerance, and a price far lower than malate or bisglycinate.

Glycinate and threonate have specific advantages — threonate, in particular, crosses the blood-brain barrier more readily and may have greater cognitive effect — but for the general use I give it (muscle relaxation and sleep quality), citrate works well without that extra cost.

Why before bed

Magnesium has a relaxing effect on the nervous system: it supports GABAergic activity (the brain's main inhibitory neurotransmitter) and reduces activity of the HPA axis (the stress axis). Taking it at night therefore makes physiological sense: it helps the transition into sleep and improves its quality, especially the deep sleep phase.

Absorption is also improved when taken with dinner or just after, with some food. Keep it separate from zinc (which competes for absorption) and from psyllium (which sweeps it along, preventing the intestine from absorbing it).

The dose I typically use is 200–300 mg of elemental magnesium. Supplement labels can be confusing: 500 mg of magnesium citrate contains approximately 75–80 mg of elemental magnesium. You need to read the breakdown. If you take too much, your intestine will let you know promptly with a mild laxative effect.
