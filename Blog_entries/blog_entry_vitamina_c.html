Vitamin C: the best-known antioxidant, an immune system staple — among other things

Why I take it, how I spread it across the day, and what the science says about its limits and possibilities.

Vitamin C is probably the most popular supplement in the world. There is still debate about dose and effect: megadoses of several grams a day as a shield against everything?

I take it in a specific way, with a clear rationale behind it. Here I explain what that is.

What vitamin C does in the body

Ascorbic acid — the technical name for vitamin C — is an essential water-soluble antioxidant. Unlike fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), it is not stored in fatty tissue: whatever is not used is excreted in urine within a few hours. That has two important implications: first, the risk of toxicity is low compared to other vitamins; second, a single large dose is less effective than several smaller doses spread throughout the day.

Its most relevant functions for my context:

  • Collagen synthesis: essential for the integrity of skin, blood vessels, cartilage and connective tissue.
  • Immune function: involved in the activation and migration of neutrophils and lymphocytes, the front-line cells of the immune system.
  • Non-haem iron absorption: taken alongside plant foods rich in iron, it significantly improves absorption.
  • Antioxidant: neutralises free radicals, particularly relevant during intense exercise or periods of elevated oxidative stress.

How I use it

My approach is to spread one to three grams daily across several doses, no more than one gram at a time. The intestinal absorption capacity for vitamin C is limited: bioavailability declines with the amount taken, and the body needs a few hours between doses to make the most of it. Splitting makes physiological sense.

I try to combine it with foods naturally rich in vitamin C — kiwi, red pepper, citrus fruits, berries — whenever I can. Vitamin C in its food context comes alongside bioflavonoids and other cofactors that enhance its antioxidant activity in ways that isolated supplements do not fully replicate. If this idea interests you, look up the concept of "nutritional matrix".

An important note: high-dose vitamin C interferes with several blood tests, especially glucose and some haemoglobin assays. If you are having blood work done, stop the supplement at least 48 hours beforehand and let your doctor know.

What it does not do (or does less than claimed)

Vitamin C does not "cure" colds. That is fairly well established — but what it does appear to do is slightly shorten their duration and reduce symptom severity, especially in people under intense physical stress.

By competing with glucose molecules for absorption — a matter of molecular similarity — vitamin C also seems to produce protective effects against a range of health conditions.

Megadoses (several grams per day) appear to have positive short-term effects under medical supervision for certain treatments (it can be given intravenously to bypass intestinal absorption). However, some studies link high chronic doses to an increased risk of oxalate kidney stones in predisposed individuals. More is not always better.
