Tao para vivir (Tao for Living): when calm is not achieved, it is allowed

Ángel García, Daniel García and Liu Zheng · Ediciones Oberón / Anaya Multimedia · Available in Spanish only

There are books that teach. And there are books that remind. Tao para vivir firmly belongs to the second group. It is not an instruction manual: it is an invitation to set down what you no longer need to carry, and to rediscover something that, deep down, you never entirely lost.

I found it at a time when I had been looking for answers in the wrong direction: outward, upward, toward the next step. The Tao, with its apparently paradoxical logic, pointed me inward and downward. Toward stillness. Toward breathing.

Three voices, one tree

The book is the work of three authors who arrived at the Tao by different paths: Ángel García through spiritual seeking, Daniel García through martial arts and movement, and Professor Liu Zheng through Traditional Chinese Medicine, with over twenty years practising Qì Gong and a career as a teacher and acupuncturist in Spain.

That diversity of origin shows in the text: there are moments of philosophical reflection, others of practical instruction in Tao Yin and Chi Kung, and others of Chinese Medicine applied to the body. This is not a uniform book but a conversation between perspectives that enrich one another.

What unites them is the conviction that physical, mental, emotional and spiritual balance is not achieved by patching symptoms but by integrating every aspect of the person. And that breathing is, along that path, a guiding thread.

What I got most from it

The idea that struck me deepest is simple to state and difficult to practise: what matters is not what happens to us, but how we respond. The Tao does not promise to eliminate problems — it promises to change the relationship you have with them. And that is, perhaps, the most radical form of freedom.

I found the chapter on Tao Yin especially valuable. In every movement there is a Yang phase — the push, the exhalation — and a Yin phase — the release, the inhalation. Every breath is, literally, a complete cycle. You cannot stay indefinitely in contraction or in expansion. Learning to move fluidly between the two is learning to live.

The book also includes popular sayings — both Spanish and Taoist — along with anecdotes and personal experiences from the authors in retreats and travels. That blend keeps it close and human, far from the solemnity of other texts. The Tao, properly understood, never is solemn.

Who I recommend it to

If your exploration of breathing has led you to ask who you are when you stop running, this book may be your next step. It will not give you step-by-step instructions — the Tao does not work that way — but it will open an inner space where you can find your own rhythm.

You could read it in one sitting, though my recommendation is to keep it nearby: to return to it when you need it, to underline sentences that said nothing the first time and say everything months later. That, for me, is one of the signs of what I consider a good book.
